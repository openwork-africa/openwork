# Openwork is now open source

The goal is to eradicate Google Workspace, Microsoft Office 365, Slack, Teams, and every other tool we depend on but don't own. This is mainly about South Africa and Africa, because almost all the technology we use is hosted outside them. Openwork is a way to own our intelligence, our compute, and our conversations, for those who care about privacy.

Think about what happens when you go to work. You do the work that should be building your own country, on tools owned by a European or American company, and you leave your data and knowledge with them, unattended. Then you go to sleep. They're in a different time zone, so while you sleep, they wake up.

Competition depends on doing the work and discovering things others haven't. You get insights that are unique to your situation, and you need to act on them before someone else has the same realisation. If you give those insights away before you can use them, how are you expected to survive? In a dog-eat-dog world, you're serving yourself up on a silver platter.

A private company can make its own choices. If it trusts a corporation with its data and accepts the risk of insider trading, espionage, or theft, that's its call.

What really bothers me is our governments in Africa, and most of all South Africa, because that's where I am. They use Zoho and Microsoft every day and export all their insights. I'm not saying they have many. But one day, when we're under attack because we stood our ground, how do we defend ourselves if all our data lives off the continent? It's like telling the people who oppressed us, "Here's the key to my kingdom, please carry on."

I'm not a fan of stats, and you don't need them to see what's happening. But look at this: unemployment keeps rising while we keep going digital. We keep migrating to the internet, and we keep consuming technology we didn't build. TikTok is amazing. Google is amazing. I'm not discounting that. But we overlook what we give up by being bystanders on other people's platforms.

Companies like Google have no reason to care about our countries. When they release something "US only", that's not a betrayal. It's their country, and they're looking after it. Meanwhile we don't own an app store, so every developer pays 30% to an American company just to distribute an app, and pays another to host it. That could go on for days.

If the tools we use to go digital are run by people who don't live here, they have no reason to build technology for our context. Look at Google Maps. I've given people the same address more times than I can count, and everyone ends up somewhere different. It works perfectly in America, where it was built. The things it could do for a business go well beyond discovery, but it isn't in their interest to build that here. I'm not sure train tracks are even on it, and our cycling routes aren't. I'd have to check.

Businesses should be able to change the technology they run on. When I help organisations adopt AI, many choose Claude. Then their teams ask me for features, and I can't help, because Claude isn't my tool. It was built by an American company overseas, and I can't pick up the phone. If it were a local tool, I'd be in the same WhatsApp group saying, "I've implemented your product in all these companies, and they keep asking for this. Can you build it?" That feedback would reach the people building it, and the builder would earn more. The users would also get more value and make more money.

I could go on for days, and I probably will turn this into a series: the disadvantages of not owning our technology, of recommending Google Workspace, and of letting Microsoft train on our data. We're also fine with the phone in our pocket not being built, maintained, or operated in South Africa or Africa. We're fine with not controlling how knowledge and insight are distributed. We shouldn't be. We should treat this like a war, because it is one.

If Google and Microsoft existed within our country, they'd have a serious incentive to keep improving their technology for its citizens. A business is also supposed to put money back into the community it belongs to, but most of our digital spend leaves the country, so there's little to replenish anything.

That's why I'm building Openwork. It isn't fancy or different. It's essentially a WhatsApp clone and works like WhatsApp. What makes it different is the mission. When someone realises they're running technology that doesn't have their interests at heart, they should have somewhere to switch to. Microsoft recently went down for a whole day, and businesses worldwide stopped. You can't expect one organisation to serve the whole world without it ending in disappointment.

Open sourcing is the first step. Next, I'll keep talking about how simple a chat interface is, and about why it matters to hold your conversations on your own infrastructure and keep your data yours. I need new ways to say that, because I can't keep repeating it the same way.

Sometime next year, I'd love to run a proper developer conference, one people get as excited about as WWDC. I want a Comic-Con for Openwork. I want the excitement of a new pair of Nikes dropping, or a new Cassper Nyovest show, or a new amapiano song. I'll be fighting for that level of excitement for a long time, so bear with me while I go a bit crazy on the internet.

This first version is basic. It captures the essence of taking notes to yourself: you can send messages to yourself. It will stay like that for a while as I move more features from the private version to the open-source one. 

I implement AI in organisations, they're stuck with closed-source options(Claude, ChatGPT etc), and every request that can't be done ends up as an external tool and a terrible experience. This is also a way to enable a new wave of people implementing AI in organisations, and to let those organisations own their intelligence and their data.

I'll write a full post on why Openwork is pivotal to ending unemployment in South Africa. I'll enjoy painting the picture of what happens when we organise ourselves on our own technology and configure it to our needs, so that people can see, and act on, opportunities that exist but are invisible right now.

Openwork is the foundational technology we should have had from day one. It's hard to exploit the opportunities around us when our tools are built around other countries' economies and surroundings. Technology built from the ground up should help the people who use it discover, interact with, and exploit opportunities. That's the dream, the vision, and the goal.

Whoop, whoop.
